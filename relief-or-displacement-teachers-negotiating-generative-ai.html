Relief or Displacement? How Teachers Are Negotiating Generative AI's Role in Their Professional Practice

Core Problem and Proposed Solution

Generative AI is entering K-12 classrooms quickly, through district-level policy rollouts and industry-led teacher trainings — the standard "adopt and train" playbook used for past edtech tools. Decades of edtech research show that tools promising personalization and access often deepen inequities, because resources, training, and institutional support are never distributed evenly. Rather than asking whether genAI improves outcomes, the authors ask how teachers in a district that already adopted it are actually negotiating its role inside their existing professional practice, policy environment, and relationships with students.

Methodology

  • Design: Qualitative, interview-based study.
  • Participants: 22 teachers from a single large U.S. school district described as an early adopter of generative AI.
  • Data collection: Semi-structured interviews.
  • Analysis focus: Motivations driving adoption, factors underlying resistance, the boundaries teachers negotiate to align genAI use with their values, and the sociotechnical dynamics — district policy, professional norms, relational commitments — that shape how teachers navigate the tools' promises and risks.

Learning Framework Breakdown

To help master the core ideas, this section explains the paper using multiple learning frameworks, moving from definitions to intuition, root-cause reasoning, and practical examples.

1) Glossary of Key Terms

  • GenAI in classrooms: Tools like chatbots or AI writing assistants teachers use for lesson planning, grading, feedback, or instruction.
  • "Adopt and train" playbook: The common edtech rollout pattern — give teachers a tool, run one training session, assume adoption follows.
  • Sociotechnical dynamics: How a technology's real-world use is shaped by social structures — policy, norms, relationships — not just by the tool's technical features.
  • Relief vs. displacement: The paper's framing for two opposite reactions to the same tool — genAI taking tedious work off a teacher's plate (relief), versus genAI crowding out judgment, relationships, or professional identity the teacher values (displacement).
  • Boundary negotiation: The ongoing, personal line-drawing teachers do about which tasks they will or won't hand to AI.

2) ELI5 (Explain Like I am Five)

Imagine handing every teacher in a school district a new assistant on the same day, with one training session, and saying "use this to save time." Some teachers feel instant relief — grading goes faster, lesson plans come together quicker. Others feel like something important is quietly being replaced — the part of the job where they actually think through a lesson, or know a struggling student well enough to give feedback themselves. This paper asks real teachers, in detail, which parts felt like relief and which parts felt like something being taken away, and why.

3) The 5 Whys (Root Cause Analysis)

Goal: Understand why the same tool can feel like help to one teacher and loss to another.

  1. Why study teachers' lived experience instead of measuring whether genAI saves time or improves scores? Because the "adopt and train" playbook has failed before even when a tool measurably saved time, if it clashed with teachers' values or deepened inequity — so negotiation matters as much as raw efficiency.
  2. Why would saving a teacher time ever feel bad? Because tasks that look like "overhead" from a district's perspective — grading, feedback, lesson planning — can be exactly where a teacher's professional judgment and relationship with students actually lives.
  3. Why do teachers resist a tool meant to help them? Because resistance here isn't about disliking AI itself — it's about defending which parts of the job a teacher considers core to their professional identity versus which parts they're willing to delegate.
  4. Why does one district's early adoption matter for understanding genAI broadly? Because it's a real, in-progress rollout, not a lab study — it surfaces the actual policy, training, and peer pressures shaping adoption, which a survey of isolated AI use wouldn't capture.
  5. Why frame this as "relief or displacement" instead of just "helpful or not helpful"? Because the same genAI feature can be relief for one teacher and displacement for another, depending on what each sees as the irreplaceable core of their job — the paper studies that negotiation, not a verdict on the tool itself.

4) Example Scenarios

Scenario A: Relief

A teacher uses genAI to draft routine parent emails and generate a first-pass rubric for an assignment — tasks they consider necessary but not central to teaching. The time saved goes toward more one-on-one time with students.

  • Why it's relief: The tool absorbs "overhead" work the teacher never wanted to spend energy on, freeing time for what they see as the real job.

Scenario B: Displacement

A different teacher is asked by their district to use genAI to generate personalized feedback comments on student writing.

  • Why it's displacement: For this teacher, giving feedback is where they build a relationship with each student and notice who's struggling. Handing that to AI doesn't just save time — it removes something they see as core to the job, even if the output reads fine on paper.

Key Findings and Insights

  • Targeted motivations, not blanket enthusiasm: Teachers' reasons for adopting genAI centered on relieving specific, identifiable burdens, rather than a general embrace of AI in teaching.
  • Resistance tracked professional identity, not technophobia: Teachers' pushback mapped onto tasks they saw as central to their role or their relationship with students, not a reflexive distrust of new technology.
  • Active boundary-setting: Teachers drew and defended their own lines around which tasks they would or wouldn't delegate to genAI, guided by personal values as much as by district policy.
  • A systemic negotiation, not just an individual choice: District policy, professional norms among peers, and relational commitments to students all shaped how individual teachers navigated genAI.
  • A challenge to the standard playbook: The findings echo earlier edtech research that "adopt and train" rollouts of tools promising personalization and access can deepen inequity when resources and support are distributed unevenly.

Practical Applications

  • District policy: GenAI rollout plans should account for teacher-level boundary-setting and professional identity, not just provide training and assume uniform adoption.
  • Tool design: Features for teachers could be designed to visibly preserve teacher judgment in tasks like feedback, rather than fully automating them, to avoid triggering a displacement reaction.
  • Equity: Because uneven resources and institutional support shaped past edtech failures, districts should watch for genAI widening disparities between schools or teachers with different levels of support, not just track adoption rates.
  • Research: This interview-based approach — tracking how people themselves narrate relief versus displacement — offers a template for studying other AI-in-the-workplace rollouts, not just classrooms.

Caveats

The study covers 22 teachers from a single large U.S. school district that was an early adopter of genAI, so findings reflect one district's specific policy and training context rather than a representative sample of all teachers. As a qualitative interview study, it's designed to surface the range and texture of teachers' negotiation, not to measure how common any one pattern is across teachers generally.
