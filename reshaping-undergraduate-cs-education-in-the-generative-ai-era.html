Reshaping Undergraduate Computer Science Education in the Generative AI Era

Core Problem and Proposed Solution

Generative AI now automates much of the implementation work — coding, debugging, testing, documentation — that undergraduate CS curricula were historically built to teach and assess. This report, from two international NUS–Google workshops, argues that framing CS education mainly around hands-on implementation is becoming less tenable, and proposes shifting the emphasis toward understanding, directing, and critically verifying AI-generated artifacts, while preserving the fundamentals (abstraction, system design, algorithmic reasoning) that AI doesn't remove the need for.

Methodology

This is a qualitative, consensus-based workshop report, not an empirical study. Faculty, industry practitioners, and students took part in two NUS–Google workshops in Singapore. The authors describe no surveys, sample sizes, or formal statistical analysis — the findings reflect expert discussion and synthesis across the 23 authors, not measured outcomes.

Learning Framework Breakdown

To help master the core ideas, this section explains the paper using multiple learning frameworks, moving from definitions to intuition, root-cause reasoning, and practical examples.

1) Glossary of Key Terms

  • AI-native competencies: Skills for working effectively with AI tools directly, such as prompting, directing, and correcting AI output.
  • Breadcrumbs (pedagogy): Small nudges and exercises woven into existing courses to keep foundational skills (debugging, reasoning about correctness) alive even as AI handles routine implementation.
  • Verification-centric assessment: Grading a student's ability to check, explain, and fix an artifact (including one AI generated), not just whether the artifact works.
  • Re-centering fundamentals: Deliberately keeping core CS theory (abstraction, algorithms, system design) at the heart of the curriculum rather than letting it erode as implementation gets automated.
  • Workshop / white paper: A report based on expert discussion and consensus, distinct from an empirical study with measured outcomes.

2) ELI5 (Explain Like I am Five)

Imagine learning to drive in a world where cars can mostly drive themselves. You'd still need to know how engines work, how to read a map, and what to do when the self-driving system makes a mistake. This paper asks what driving school should teach when the car can drive itself — and answers: less time on manual mechanics, more time on judgment, oversight, and knowing when something's wrong.

3) The 5 Whys (Root Cause Analysis)

Goal: Understand why CS curricula need rethinking now rather than just adding an AI elective.

  1. Why rethink the CS curriculum now? Because generative AI already does much of what intro-to-advanced CS courses trained students to do by hand.
  2. Why not just add an "AI tools" elective and keep everything else the same? Because the automated tasks (implementation, debugging, documentation) were the backbone of how skills like system design were taught and assessed in the first place.
  3. Why keep teaching abstraction and system design if AI can write the code? Because someone still has to decide what to build, verify it's correct, and catch AI's mistakes — none of that is automated.
  4. Why use small "breadcrumb" exercises instead of overhauling every course? Because full curriculum redesign is slow and institutionally risky, while breadcrumbs are incremental nudges that can be added to existing courses now.
  5. Why does this need institutional support, not just new course content? Because assessment design, advising, and infrastructure all assume the old implementation-centric model and have to change alongside the courses.

4) Example Scenario

A data structures course used to grade students mainly on "did you correctly implement a balanced tree." Under the proposed shift, it also grades: given an AI-generated balanced-tree implementation with a subtle bug, can the student find it, explain why it's wrong, and fix it? Same topic, shifted emphasis — from producing the implementation to verifying and correcting one.

Key Findings and Insights

The workshops converged on five areas for curriculum reform:

  • AI-native competencies: Explicitly teach working with AI — prompting, directing, and verifying its output — as a core skill, not an elective add-on.
  • Re-centering fundamental education: Protect time spent on abstraction, algorithms, and system design, since AI automates implementation but not the judgment behind it.
  • Enhancing advanced pathways: Give students who move faster through basics deeper specialization opportunities.
  • Embracing new pedagogies: Use incremental "breadcrumb" exercises inside existing courses to preserve foundational skills.
  • Shifting institutional support: Adjust advising, assessment design, and infrastructure to match the new curriculum emphasis.

Practical Applications

  • Low-cost starting point: Departments can begin with breadcrumb exercises inside current courses rather than attempting a full redesign immediately.
  • Assessment redesign: Shift some grading toward verifying and critiquing AI-generated output, not just producing working code.
  • Planning input: Useful for CS curriculum committees as a discussion framework, not as a validated pedagogy with outcome data.

Caveats

Unlike the empirical studies elsewhere in this library, this is a workshop consensus white paper: it reports expert judgment and proposed direction, with no quantitative results, effect sizes, or measured outcomes behind the five reform areas.
